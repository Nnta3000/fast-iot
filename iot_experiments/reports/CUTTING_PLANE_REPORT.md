# Cutting-plane inverse OT — classic Kelley cuts with one OT solve per cut

**Status: CURRENT, empirical.** This is a *new* method arm, not a replacement for the shipped
exact solver. Method label: `cutting-plane-ot-oracle`.

This report answers the question "does the $K$-dimensional cutting-plane reformulation keep the
number of OT solves small and flat in $n$?" It does, at $K \le 16$; the wall-clock win requires a
near-linear OT oracle (HELLO), not POT. All numbers below are single-seed wall times on one
machine; they are for relative comparison, not a benchmark submission.

## 1. What the method is (and which family it belongs to)

It belongs to the **exact family**, not the gradient family. The exact master and this master
describe the same admissible cone

$$
\theta \text{ admissible} \iff \langle g_P,\theta\rangle \le 0 \ \ \forall P\in\mathrm{vert}\,\Pi(a,b),
\qquad g_P := \Phi^\top(\hat\pi - P),
$$

once the exact master's $(f,g)$ are projected out (Benders / partial dualisation). The exact
method generates rows over **edges** ($\sim 3.4(m+n)$ of them plus $(f,g)$); this method generates
rows over **OT vertices** and keeps a bank of them. Per-iteration cost is gradient-like (one OT
solve), the guarantee is exact-like (termination with a certificate from the OT plan). The master
is the classic Kelley LP

$$
\texttt{max\_margin:}\quad \max_{\theta\in\Theta,\,m\ge 0} m \ \text{ s.t. } \langle g_s,\theta\rangle + m \le 0,
\qquad
\texttt{kelley:}\quad \min_{\theta\in\Theta}\max_s \langle g_s,\theta\rangle .
$$

Both are LPs in $K+1$ variables with $t$ rows, built with
`src/iot/cutting_plane.py::solve_cut_master`; the loop is `solve_cutting_plane`. The file uses
only the existing `FeatureMap` / `LinearThetaSet` seams, never materialises $m\times n$, and
reuses `subgradient.{observed_feature_moment,support_moment,make_pot_solver}`.

The basic proof (as given in the theory note) is: each OT solve returns a valid subgradient, so
$\theta^*$ satisfies every cut; the loop is finite; and a volume/Grünbaum argument gives
$T = O(K\ln(n\phi_{\max}\mathrm{diam}\,\Theta/\delta^*))$. Two caveats are load-bearing and are
**not** proved here: (i) the Grünbaum bound assumes **centroid** queries, whereas the shipped
master returns a max-margin LP vertex, so the bound is a prediction, not a theorem; (ii) the
$1/n$ vertex-mass step assumes uniform marginals and a permutation $\hat\pi$. What *is* checked
in tests is the separation soundness: `tests/iot_cutting_plane_test.py` verifies that $\theta^*$
satisfies every generated cut and that the gradient is exactly the feature-moment difference.

## 3. $n$-scaling — cut count is flat in $n$

OT solves to reach $L_0=0$ (every run `converged=True`, `is_optimal=True`, `final_gap=0`):

| $n$ | $K=4$ calls | $K=8$ calls | $K=8$ wall s | $K=8$ s/solve |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 200 | 22 | 59 | 0.33 | 0.006 |
| 800 | 26 | 79 | 7.62 | 0.096 |
| 1600 | 33 | 83 | 29.3 | 0.353 |
| 3200 | 31 | 84 | 140.2 | 1.669 |

$K=4$ is flat over a $16\times$ range in $n$ (22 → 31); $K=8$ rises once (59 → 84) and then
plateaus. Both are consistent with the predicted logarithmic (not polynomial) $n$-dependence.
The growth is **in $K$, not in $n$**.

## 4. $K$-scaling — the real cost driver

At $n=200$:

| $K$ | 4 | 8 | 16 | 32 | 64 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| OT solves | 22 | 59 | 90 | 282 | 710 |

Roughly linear in $K$ from $K=16$ up. This is the predicted signature and it matches the earlier
verdict: $K\le 16$–$32$ is attractive (tens of cuts), $K=64$ already needs hundreds.

## 5. Normalisation ablation

Row-normalising the cuts does not change the halfspaces, only the master's geometry. It roughly
halves the cut count:

| instance | unnormalised | normalised |
|---|---|---|
| $n=800,K=8$ | 131 | 79 |
| $n=200,K=16$ | 240 | 90 |
| $n=800,K=16$ | 314 | 155 |

## 6. Head-to-head (clean, $K=8$, seed 0)

| method | $n$ | OT solves / outer iters | wall s | s/solve | reported gap (exact: $\delta$; others: $L_0$) | `is_optimal` |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| cutting-plane | 200 | 59 | 0.33 | 0.006 | 0 | yes |
| exact | 200 | 13 | 0.57 | 0.044 | $\delta=-8.0\text{e-}5$ | yes |
| subgradient (`inv_sqrt`, lr 0.5) | 200 | 480 | 2.05 | 0.004 | 0 | yes |
| cutting-plane | 800 | 79 | 7.91 | 0.100 | 0 | yes |
| exact | 800 | 16 | 6.71 | 0.420 | $\delta=-7.9\text{e-}5$ | yes |
| subgradient (`inv_sqrt`, lr 0.5) | 800 | 314 | 30.45 | 0.097 | 0 | yes |

Against the **tuned** subgradient the cutting plane needs $8\times$ fewer OT solves at $n=200$
(59 vs 480) and $4\times$ fewer at $n=800$ (79 vs 314), with no step size to tune.

Against the **exact** method the picture is different: exact converges in 13–16 outer iterations
while cutting-plane needs 59–79. Exact's per-iteration cost is $\sim 4.4\times$ higher (its master
LP grows as $n^{2.1}$ and its oracle scan is $O(mnK)$), so the two wash out **with POT** ($n=200$:
0.33 s vs 0.57 s to the cutting plane; $n=800$: 7.91 s vs 6.71 s to exact). The cutting-plane
master itself is negligible: measured median solve time is 1.4 ms ($t=84,K=8$) and 27 ms
($t=400,K=64$), i.e. $\le 0.1\%$ of a $n=3200$ POT solve.

## 7. Findings

1. **The cutting-plane premise holds at small $K$.** Cut counts are tens and flat in $n$ over
   $n=200\ldots3200$ ($K=4$: 22–31; $K=8$: 59–84). This is the property the whole argument needed.
2. **$K$ is the cost.** Cuts grow roughly linearly in $K$ (90 at $K=16$, 282 at $K=32$, 710 at
   $K=64$). At $K=64$ the method is not competitive.
3. **With POT the wall time is a wash against exact; the method's payoff is the oracle swap.**
   The exact method's bottleneck (the $n^{2.1}$ master LP + dense scan) is removed, and the master
   becomes milliseconds. But POT's dense $O(n^2)$ oracle then dominates, so the $5\times$ cheaper
   iteration is cancelled by the $5\times$ larger iteration count. Replacing POT with HELLO (the
   near-linear diagonal-weight oracle) is the decisive next experiment: the same 79 solves would
   cost a fraction of 7.7 s, whereas the exact method cannot use HELLO for its separation scan.
4. **Step rule still matters for the gradient baseline.** Within `inv_sqrt`, learning rate
   $0.5$ needs 480 solves at $n=200$ while $1.0$ needs 1719; `constant` stalls and `polyak`
   needs 1999. The cutting plane has only a tolerance, and the comparison above uses the
   *tuned* subgradient so it is not flattered by a badly chosen step rule.

## 8. Caveats / what would break

- **Noisy plans.** The master is infeasible when no $\theta$ makes $\hat\pi$ optimal. The loop
  raises `InverseOTError`; there is no silent fallback. A slack/level-bundle variant is the fix
  and is the version to build if noisy data is in scope.
- **Large $K$.** The $\Omega(K)$ lower bound for black-box localisation is visible in the data;
  do not claim the near-linear story above $K\approx32$.
- **Tie-breaks/degeneracy.** Any optimal plan gives a valid cut, so degeneracy is safe, but the
  cut count may be tie-break sensitive; only one tie-break (POT's) was tested.
- **The proof is a prediction, not a certificate.** The max-margin LP vertex is not the centroid,
  so Grünbaum's $1/e$ volume bound does not apply; the reported $n$-flatness is empirical.
  The $\delta^*$ in the bound is not measured here and is the quantity to track next.
- **Single seed, unpinned threads.** Medians/IQR over seeds and a HELLO arm are required before
  any of this is a benchmark row.
